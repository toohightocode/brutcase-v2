Chapter I — 1952

THE
PROTOTYPE

Marseille, France. A vertical city for 1,600 souls, raised on pilotis above the earth, its concrete skin bearing the memory of the timber that shaped it.

Le Corbusier called it Unité d'Habitation — the Housing Unit. But it was more than housing. It was a manifesto cast in reinforced concrete, 18 stories of communal living where the corridors became streets and the roof became a landscape. The concrete was not hidden, not painted, not ashamed. It was béton brut — raw, honest, unflinching.

The building breathed. The modulor governed its proportions. Children played on the roof terrace where concrete sculptural forms rose like geological formations. This was not merely architecture — it was social engineering made visible, the welfare state given physical form, democracy poured into molds and left to cure in the Mediterranean sun.

Chapter II — 1953

THE
SMITHSONS

Alison and Peter Smithson. A marriage of minds that would redefine British architecture. They did not build much, but what they built changed everything.

1953

Alison Smithson publishes the first usage of "new brutalism" in Architectural Design. The plan for their unbuilt Soho house appears — a house that would have exposed its structure entirely, "without interior finishes wherever practicable." The building was never built. The idea was unstoppable.

1954

Hunstanton School opens in Norfolk. Inspired by Mies van der Rohe's Alumni Memorial Hall in Chicago, it becomes the first completed building in the world to carry the title "new brutalist" by its own architects. Philip Johnson declares it "the most truly modern building in England." The water tank, normally hidden, stands proud as a tower. Pipes and conduits run visible along corridors. Nothing is concealed. Everything is honest.

Chapter III — 1955

THE
CRITIC

Reyner Banham was not an architect. He was a historian with the soul of a poet and the precision of a scientist. In 1955, he published The New Brutalism — an essay that would become the movement's bible.

Banham gave brutalism its three commandments: Formal legibility of plan. Clear exhibition of structure. Valuation of materials for their inherent qualities 'as found'. He linked the Smithsons' ethic to Le Corbusier's béton brut and Dubuffet's art brut — raw art, raw concrete, raw truth.

"If there is one single verbal formula that has made the concept of Brutalism admissible in most of the world's Western languages, it is that Le Corbusier himself described that concrete work as 'béton-brut'."

Reyner Banham, 1966

In 1966, Banham expanded his thesis into a book: The New Brutalism: Ethic or Aesthetic? The question mark was deliberate. He knew that what the Smithsons had created was not merely a look — it was a way of seeing. A moral position. The woodness of the wood. The sandiness of the sand. The concreteness of concrete.

Chapter IV — The Golden Age

CONCRETE CATHEDRALS

1965

BARBICAN

Chamberlin, Powell & Bon. London. A city within a city — towers, terraces, lakes, a fortress of culture rising from the ashes of the Blitz. The largest arts center in Europe, wrapped in concrete that refuses to pretend it is anything else.

London, UK
1976

NATIONAL THEATRE

Denys Lasdun. The South Bank. Tiered concrete terraces cascading toward the Thames like a ziggurat for the modern age. "A building should be read like a geography," Lasdun said. And so it is — a landscape of board-marked concrete, shadow and light, drama made permanent.

London, UK
1968

BOSTON CITY HALL

Kallmann, McKinnell & Knowles. The building that dared to look like what it was — government as geology, democracy as fortress. Its overhanging volumes and shadowed voids spoke of civic power without classical columns or marble pretense.

Boston, USA

THE SOCIALIST DREAM

In the Eastern Bloc, brutalism found its ideological home. Concrete emphasized equality — no marble for the bosses, no ornament for the bourgeoisie. Paneláky rose across Czechoslovakia. The Genex Tower pierced the Belgrade sky. Soviet architects trained Vietnamese builders. From Skopje to Hanoi, the language of raw concrete became the architecture of utopia.

THE CAMPUS REVOLUTION

Universities became brutalism's laboratories. Essex, East Anglia, York, Durham — each campus a concrete experiment in communal living. The ziggurats of Lasdun. The beehives of Oxford. The History Faculty at Cambridge, where James Stirling turned education into a geometric poem. Knowledge, poured in concrete, left to weather and age with dignity.

Chapter V — The Return

NEOBRUTALISM

2015

Brutal London is published. This Brutal World follows. Instagram accounts dedicated to concrete gain millions of followers. The children of the demolition era grow up and wonder what was lost.

2018

The Atlas of Brutalist Architecture documents over 850 buildings across 102 countries. Preston Bus Station, twice threatened with demolition, gains Grade II listed status. Brutalism has finally come of age.

2020

Grafton Architects win the Pritzker Prize for their neobrutalist UTEC campus in Lima. The citation praises "an urban icon" that exposes its structure with the same honesty the Smithsons demanded seventy years before.

"The battle has been won and brutalism has finally come of age."

Catherine Croft, Twentieth Century Society
Epilogue

THE WOODNESS
OF THE WOOD

Peter Smithson believed brutalism was not concerned with the material as such but rather the quality of material. The woodness of the wood. The sandiness of the sand. The concreteness of concrete.

After seventy years of praise and demolition, after utopia and decay, after neobrutalism and Instagram aesthetics, this remains the core: architecture that refuses to lie. That shows its bones. That stands in the rain and lets the rain show where it has been. That ages not with shame but with the dignity of stone — because concrete, at its best, is nothing less than artificial stone, cast by human hands, cured by time, honest forever.

FAMOUS STRUCTURES

1963 — Boston, USA

BOSTON
CITY
HALL

Over 50 years since its unveiling, it remains one of the most controversial Brutalist buildings in the United States. Suitably for a symbol of the political process, it is widely loved - and disliked - for its uncompromising aesthetic.

1965 — London, UK

BARBICAN
ESTATE

Rather like Le Corbusier's Unité d'Habitation, The Barbican was intended as a model of post-war urban living that aimed to be self-contained and community-centered.

1967 — Montreal, CANADA

HABITAT
67

Completed at the height of Brutalmania, in 1967, this striking modular structure of prefab concrete units stacked in unique configurations continues a recurring motif in our selection of Brutal bangers - representing an utopian, Modernist ideal of urban living. Every unit even has its own private garden and access to natural light.

1976 — London, UK

Royal
National
Theatre

A controversial structure in the heart of London's South Bank, the home of British theater has been variously described as a “nuclear power station” by King Charles, a “lovely work” by poet Sir John Betjeman and “an aesthetic of broken forms” by architectural writer Mark Girouard. Designed around the idea of an Ancient Greek amphitheater, with layered concrete terraces, the NT's modular, cubic design was shaped to maximize efficient airflow throughout, while exuding an austere air of Brutalist functionality, reminiscent of Le Corbusier.
